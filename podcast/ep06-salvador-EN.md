# Episode 6 — Salvador de Bahia
### Language: English
### Format: Interview (Host + Francesco)
### Estimated duration: ~6 minutes

---

**HOST:** Welcome back to Sailing Oroboro. After seven months in Brazil, Francesco and Yuka sailed north from Ilha Grande and Paraty — all the way up the coast to Salvador de Bahia. Francisco, why is Salvador significant?

**FRANCESCO:** Salvador is the third largest city in Brazil. It's also where Brazil's African roots are most alive — in the music, the food, the religion, the people. And it turned out to have a very personal connection for me.

**HOST:** What connection?

**FRANCESCO:** The name. I studied at the University of Florence, and one of my history courses was on the voyages of Christopher Columbus and the early explorers. Amerigo Vespucci — the Florentine explorer who the Americas were eventually named after — sighted this bay on November 1st, 1502. November 1st is All Saints' Day... "Todos os Santos." So he named it Bahia de Todos os Santos — Bay of All Saints. And the settlement became Salvador, because his family in Florence lived near the church of All Saints, also known as San Salvatore.

**HOST:** So you sailed into a bay named by someone from your city.

**FRANCESCO:** By someone whose family church I know personally. It contains a fresco by Ghirlandaio commissioned by the Vespucci family. Saint Jerome — the patron of translators, which I am. I stood in front of that fresco as a student. And twenty-five years later... I sailed into the bay that Vespucci named. That felt extraordinary.

**HOST:** The city itself — what's it like?

**FRANCESCO:** It's said there's a church for every day of the year. In reality there are "only" 180. The historic Pelourinho district is all colonial architecture, steep cobblestone streets, colour everywhere. At night, percussion drums, capoeira in the squares. The African influence is everywhere and it's beautiful.

**HOST:** You also attended a Candomblé ceremony.

**FRANCESCO:** This was important to me. Candomblé is an Afro-Brazilian religion brought over by enslaved people from West Africa. We heard that evening there was going to be a ceremony to celebrate Yemanjá — the Queen of the Ocean. The patron spirit of sailors. The feminine principle of the sea. I thought: we are sailors, crossing the ocean. We HAVE to go.

**HOST:** What was it like?

**FRANCESCO:** Not what we expected. It was... intense. Sacred. Not a performance for tourists. Real. We were outsiders and we knew it. But we were welcomed. And standing there as a sailor, in a ceremony dedicated to the spirit of the ocean you've been crossing for months... there's something profound about that.

**HOST:** You also had guests on board in Salvador.

**FRANCESCO:** First Karine, a friend from Guadeloupe. She and Yuka took a capoeira lesson together. They also spent time with local women in traditional Bahian dress — those extraordinary layered white skirts, African jewelry. The women told them they needed to eat more. They didn't know Yuka and Karine very well.

**HOST:** And then Mario.

**FRANCESCO:** Mario is a special case. Two years earlier, Yuka and I were in Miami at a boat show — still deciding whether to actually buy the boat. We were wavering. Mario was with us. He's the one who said: "Do it. Pull the trigger." Without Mario, there is no Oroboro. There is no voyage. And here he is... nineteen years old... on board, in Salvador, about to sail four hundred miles to Recife with us.

**HOST:** How was that passage?

**FRANCESCO:** Brutal. Strong headwinds, 2.5 metre waves on the bow, squalls with gusts up to 27 knots. The boat was slamming into the sea the whole way. Mario had never done anything like it. But when you're nineteen... nothing scares you. He was fine. Better than fine.

**HOST:** Tell me about the favela lunch.

**FRANCESCO:** I was working on the boat at the marina when a local sailor asked me if I wanted to join him for lunch. I said yes. I didn't ask where. We got into his dinghy... and he took me to a restaurant in the nearby favela. Just a family cooking in a small room. Fantastic food. We were eating, talking, and then — a loud blast from the water. I turned around. Someone had dropped a bomb in the bay. To kill fish. I watched people jump from boats, from shore, even from the windows of the houses, into the water, to collect whatever came to the surface. I had never seen anything like it in my life.

**HOST:** You didn't plan that lunch.

**FRANCESCO:** I absolutely did not plan that lunch. But that's Brazil. The best things there — you never plan them.

**HOST:** Next episode: the final stretch north — crossing the equator for the first time, and the beginning of the Caribbean. This is Sailing Oroboro.
