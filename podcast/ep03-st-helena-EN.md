# Episode 3 — The Skeleton Coast & St. Helena
### Language: English
### Format: Interview (Host + Francesco)
### Estimated duration: ~8 minutes

---

**HOST:** Welcome back to Sailing Oroboro. Last episode we met the boat — Oroboro, the Leopard 40. And at the end of that episode, December 15th, 2018 — you cast off from Cape Town. Where were you actually going? Because most boats sailing from Cape Town head straight across the Atlantic.

**FRANCESCO:** Right. The classic route is Cape Town straight to St. Helena — 1,700 miles, no stops. But we chose to go north first. Up the Namibian coast. Through the Skeleton Coast. A detour most sailors skip entirely.

**HOST:** Why?

**FRANCESCO:** A friend in South Africa — Allan — had told me about a ghost town in the Namibian desert. A diamond-mining town that was abandoned in the 1950s and left for the sand to swallow. I'd been to ghost towns in California and Nevada on motorcycle trips. Something about them pulls at me. So when I found out we could sail there... I put it on the navigation plan and didn't ask too many questions.

**HOST:** The Skeleton Coast. Let's start there. The name alone is ominous.

**FRANCESCO:** It earned the name. The Benguela Current — this enormous cold current flowing north along the coast of southern Africa — creates two things: extreme fog and brutal surf. Ships that wrecked on this coast couldn't be reached from land. The desert goes on for hundreds of miles in every direction. If you ran aground here, the bones of the ship just... stayed. Hence the name.

**HOST:** And you sailed right along it.

**FRANCESCO:** We hugged the coast deliberately. The current helped push us north — about half a knot of free speed. The fog was thick some nights. But we had one stretch of perfect clear weather, and the view was surreal. Desert dunes, four hundred meters high, running straight into the Atlantic. Golden sand meeting dark ocean. Nothing else. No towns, no lights, no other boats. I've only seen light that beautiful in one other place.

**HOST:** Where?

**FRANCESCO:** Venice. Same quality of surreal, impossible light. Two completely different worlds that somehow share something. I don't know how else to explain it.

**HOST:** And then Lüderitz.

**FRANCESCO:** We arrived at night — 9:30 pm. You don't usually enter an unfamiliar port after dark. But we had no choice. The port control on the VHF was fantastic — talked us in, told us exactly where to anchor. Next morning we went ashore to check in with customs and immigration. Carbon copy paper. A rubber stamp. The most relaxed officials I've encountered anywhere in the world.

**HOST:** What IS Lüderitz?

**FRANCESCO:** That's the thing — it surprises you completely. It's a small German colonial town, right on the edge of the Namib Desert. Art Nouveau buildings, colourful facades... it looks like someone picked up a neighbourhood from Hamburg and dropped it on the Skeleton Coast. It's perfectly preserved, a little strange, and completely charming.

**HOST:** And then Kolmanskop.

**FRANCESCO:** The ghost town. We organized to get there at sunrise — better light for photos. Driver met us at the jetty at 6:15 am, twenty minutes through the desert, and then suddenly: a town. Houses, a hospital, a ballroom... half-buried in sand. The desert reclaiming everything, room by room, year by year.

**HOST:** What's the history?

**FRANCESCO:** In 1908 a railway worker named August Stauch was clearing sand off the tracks near Lüderitz when he found diamonds. Not buried — on the surface, catching the moonlight. A rush followed. At the peak, 1,200 people lived there. The hospital had the FIRST X-ray machine in the southern hemisphere — not for medicine, for checking that nobody was smuggling diamonds out inside their body. Five million carats in seven years. Then the field started to deplete. By the early fifties, the last people left. And the desert moved in.

**HOST:** What does it feel like walking through it?

**FRANCESCO:** Our friend Ligia — she'd circumnavigated twice, she'd dived wrecks all over the world — she said it felt like exploring a shipwreck. Only you could take your time. No pressure of air supply. I organized everybody to get there at first light, and everybody was blown away. I looked at Francesco walking through the sand-filled rooms with a map in his hand... like Bartolomeu Dias exploring a new coast.

**HOST:** From there you kept going north. Walvis Bay for Christmas.

**FRANCESCO:** We arrived on Christmas Eve in thick fog. And when the fog cleared, the welcoming committee was... not what we expected. A giant pelican landed on the helm station roof. A seal — from Customs, apparently — wanted to board and inspect. Walvis Bay has a colony of tens of thousands of fur seals. They treat the harbour as their own. We had a seal on the dock next to us for three days.

**HOST:** From Walvis Bay — you depart for St. Helena January 1st.

**FRANCESCO:** Captain's Executive Order: midnight was declared at sundown, the champagne was opened, and we started the new year at sea. Twenty-five days offshore. 1,700 miles. Just the two of us.

**HOST:** And St. Helena.

**FRANCESCO:** You see it from miles away — volcanic rock, cliffs straight up from the water, rising out of nothing. We picked up our mooring on January 9th. And I stood in the cockpit and thought: we actually crossed an ocean.

**HOST:** Most people don't know St. Helena exists.

**FRANCESCO:** Four thousand people, British territory, in the middle of nowhere. Famous for one thing: Napoleon. After he escaped his first exile on Elba — off Italy — the British needed somewhere he absolutely could NOT escape from a second time. St. Helena was the answer. 1,200 miles from the nearest continent in every direction. Six years of his life there. Then he died.

**HOST:** You visited Longwood House.

**FRANCESCO:** His bedroom. The room where he died. Simple, modest, a little damp. The contrast between who he was — Emperor of half of Europe — and where he ended up... it's sobering. History is full of these moments. You stand in a place and feel the weight of it.

**HOST:** Jonathan.

**FRANCESCO:** Jonathan! The giant tortoise. Born around 1832. Already fully grown when he arrived on the island in 1882. Still alive today — the oldest known living land animal on Earth. He lives in the grounds of the Governor's residence and just... grazes. Completely indifferent to you. I stood at the fence watching him for a long time. He was alive for both World Wars. The moon landing. All of it. Eating grass.

**HOST:** Last question — the moonbow. That's from the next leg, technically. But...

**FRANCESCO:** It belongs here. Day 8 of the passage from St. Helena to Brazil. It was late at night. Yuka was asleep. I was alone on watch, somewhere in the deep South Atlantic — no land for over a thousand miles in any direction. The sky cleared between squalls, and I saw this arc of pale light. White. Faint. From one horizon to the other. A moonbow. The same physics as a rainbow, but the moon as the light source — 400,000 times dimmer than the sun. Your eye can't pick up the colour. You just see this perfect white arc over the ocean, in the middle of the night, in the middle of nowhere.

**HOST:** What did you do?

**FRANCESCO:** I stood there for a long time. Then I wrote two lines in the logbook: *"Also, in between squalls during the night I saw a very rare moon rainbow."* Then got back to sail trim and water calculations. The watermaker had broken three days earlier. We were rationing water. There was work to do.

**HOST:** That's ocean sailing.

**FRANCESCO:** You see something extraordinary and then you get back to work. But you carry it. Next episode: we reach Brazil. This is Sailing Oroboro.
