# Episode 4 — St. Helena to Brazil: The Longest Leg
### Language: English
### Format: Interview (Host + Francesco)
### Estimated duration: ~7 minutes

---

**HOST:** Welcome back to Sailing Oroboro. Last episode we were in St. Helena — Napoleon's island, Jonathan the tortoise, Jacob's Ladder. And then it was time to leave. Francesco, what's the next leg from St. Helena?

**FRANCESCO:** Brazil. 2,271 nautical miles. No stops in between. That was the longest single leg of the entire voyage.

**HOST:** How long at sea?

**FRANCESCO:** Eighteen days. We left January 14th. Just the two of us, again. Two thousand nautical miles of open ocean, no land, no other boats. Just... ocean.

**HOST:** What were conditions like at the start?

**FRANCESCO:** Light winds at first. Pleasant sailing. Dolphins off the bow as we left the island. Beautiful sunset... starring night. You think: this is going to be fine.

**HOST:** And then?

**FRANCESCO:** Day three. The watermaker breaks.

**HOST:** What does that mean, exactly — in the middle of the ocean?

**FRANCESCO:** The watermaker is how we make fresh water from the sea. It's not optional out there. And ours started leaking from the pump. We had 800 liters in the tanks. Enough... if we were very careful. And if we couldn't fix it, we needed to make it to Brazil on what we had.

**HOST:** Did you try to fix it?

**FRANCESCO:** I spent EIGHT HOURS in a locker under the bed disassembling the pump. On a moving boat, in the tropics. Hands and knees in a tiny space. I suspected a cracked O-ring, replaced it... put it all back together... still leaking. Beyond repair. We needed a new part. Which was somewhere in Brazil.

**HOST:** So you start rationing.

**FRANCESCO:** From that moment on — showers on the trampoline with seawater. Rinse with fresh water on the stern. Every liter counted.

**HOST:** How do you keep morale up when that happens?

**FRANCESCO:** Fishing. Day four — we caught a 14 kilogram skipjack tuna. Yuka made sashimi for lunch. Life is... suddenly better. You're in the middle of the ocean with a broken watermaker, but you're eating fresh tuna sashimi. It's a strange life.

**HOST:** What does night watch feel like — eighteen days of it?

**FRANCESCO:** Three hours on, six hours off, around the clock. At night... it's extraordinary. No light pollution for a thousand miles in any direction. Venus and Jupiter bright on the stern. The Southern Cross to port. Perseus, Andromeda, Orion's Belt to starboard. I imagined those ancient sailors who crossed these same waters with no GPS, no radio... just the stars. And a sextant. Which I also had, actually.

**HOST:** You navigated by sextant?

**FRANCESCO:** I practiced with it. Not for primary navigation — we had GPS. But I wanted to know HOW. Because if everything fails... you need the stars. And out there, taking a sight with a sextant feels completely different from reading about it in a book.

**HOST:** What was the strangest thing that happened on the passage?

**FRANCESCO:** Day fifteen. We spot a sailboat on our port side, sailing north. The skipper calls us on VHF. It's an American... sailing single-handed... and he tells us he's in FOURTH PLACE in the Golden Globe Race.

**HOST:** The solo around-the-world race. No electronics.

**FRANCESCO:** No GPS, no watermaker, no autopilot — his had been broken since the beginning. He'd been at sea for SIX MONTHS. Alone. He sounded exhausted. His speed log had been eaten by a shark in the Indian Ocean. He was navigating by sextant only, with an almanac from the wrong year. And he was crossing the Atlantic. Alone. In fourth place.

**HOST:** And you thought YOUR passage was hard.

**FRANCESCO:** Exactly. We wished him luck and carried on. I thought about him for days.

**HOST:** When does Brazil appear?

**FRANCESCO:** Day seventeen. Late at night. The Cabo Frio lighthouse. I remember standing at the helm in the dark... and there it is. A flashing light on the horizon. Land. After eighteen days of nothing but ocean... a light. I can't describe what that feels like.

**HOST:** And then?

**FRANCESCO:** We pass Rio de Janeiro in the morning. Copacabana beach off to port. We get cellular coverage for the first time in weeks. The first WhatsApp message to family. We anchor in a protected bay before sunset, surrounded by jungle... and we swim. For the first time in eighteen days. Just float in warm Brazilian water and look up at the sky.

**HOST:** What was your first impression of Brazil?

**FRANCESCO:** Ilha Grande — 365 islands, 2,000 white sandy beaches. Lush tropical forest dropping into the sea. You sail into it and you think: we were told to be afraid of this country. Everyone said don't go to Brazil. And you look around and think... this is one of the most beautiful places on Earth.

**HOST:** What about the people?

**FRANCESCO:** The people ARE Brazil. The warmth, the smiles, the sound of the language. We spoke Portuñol — a mix of Spanish and Portuguese — and somehow got around. Yuka, with her tan and her smile, was constantly mistaken for Brazilian. They'd speak to me in Portuguese... not understand... then turn to HER and ask her to translate. She loved it.

**HOST:** How long did you stay?

**FRANCESCO:** Seven months. We were supposed to stay three. But Brazil does that to you. Next episode: Paraty — one of the most perfectly preserved colonial towns in the world. And an opera singer at anchor. This is Sailing Oroboro.
