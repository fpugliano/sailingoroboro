# Episode 5 — Paraty & The Opera Singer
### Language: English
### Format: Interview (Host + Francesco)
### Estimated duration: ~6 minutes

---

**HOST:** Welcome back to Sailing Oroboro. Last episode — eighteen days at sea, a broken watermaker, water rationing, a Golden Globe racer in the middle of the Atlantic. And then Brazil. Seven months, as it turned out. Francesco, where did you go first?

**FRANCESCO:** Ilha Grande. 365 islands, 2,000 white sandy beaches. We anchored in one place after another, each one more beautiful than the last. On day one, Yuka looked around and said: "Forget the Caribbean. I want to stay here for two years." She meant it.

**HOST:** But you had a visa deadline.

**FRANCESCO:** Three months, extendable to six. Brazil does not make it easy to stay. Which is a shame, because the place makes you want to never leave.

**HOST:** You also found your first sailing companions there.

**FRANCESCO:** Plan B. A Leopard 45 — so slightly bigger than us — owned by Joaquin and Monica, a Spanish couple. We'd actually met them in Cape Town. They'd taken delivery of their boat in South Africa too, just a few months before us. We kept running into each other all the way up the Brazilian coast. Eventually we sailed together all the way to the Caribbean.

**HOST:** You were a little fleet.

**FRANCESCO:** Exactly. When you're offshore, meeting another boat that's doing the same voyage is... it's hard to explain. You speak the same language. You understand each other immediately. Joaquin and Monica became real friends.

**HOST:** Then Paraty. Tell me about Paraty.

**FRANCESCO:** Paraty means "river of fish" in the Tupi language. Founded by the Portuguese in 1667. When gold was discovered in the mountains of Minas Gerais, Paraty became the main port for exporting it to Rio. The "Caminho do Ouro" — the gold trail — connected the town to Ouro Preto in the mountains. And then... gold ran out, roads were built elsewhere, and Paraty was left behind. Which is why nothing changed.

**HOST:** What does it look like?

**FRANCESCO:** The historic centre is paved with large cobblestones — not flat, not easy to walk on. Pedestrians only. And at spring tides, the sea level rises and floods the streets through small openings in the seawalls. The water comes in... for an hour or so, shops put out boards so people can walk across... and then it drains away again. Like Venice. But smaller. And with bossa nova playing from every restaurant.

**HOST:** You made it your base.

**FRANCESCO:** We did. From Paraty we could sail to countless anchorages and beaches — both on the coast and in Ilha Grande. We'd go out for a few days, come back, provision, go out again. Perfect cruising ground.

**HOST:** What did you eat?

**FRANCESCO:** Moqueca. A Brazilian seafood stew — fish or shrimp in coconut milk, palm oil, tomatoes, onions, coriander. Served in a clay pot, still bubbling. It became our favorite dish in Brazil. And cachaça — sugarcane spirit. There's a distillery in Paraty. Mandatory stop. Absolutely mandatory.

**HOST:** Now — the opera singer. Tell me that story.

**FRANCESCO:** We were anchored in Praia dos Meros, a small cove in Ilha Grande. Just a few boats. We'd reunited with Plan B there. On board Plan B that night were two friends visiting: Ricardo and Isabel. Ricardo had circumnavigated the globe on a wooden monohull. He also played guitar — and he sang in a style called Habaneras. Spanish, very melodic, very old.

**HOST:** After dinner...

**FRANCESCO:** After dinner, Ricardo took out the guitar and started singing. It was dark — no moon. Just stars. And the music carries across the water in an anchorage. And then... from the only other boat anchored in the cove... a voice answered. A soprano. A professional Argentine opera singer, replying to each Habanera with an aria. Ricardo sings. She answers. Back and forth. Neither of them knowing the other was there until the music started.

**HOST:** You couldn't have planned that.

**FRANCESCO:** You couldn't plan that in a hundred years. We sat in the cockpit in the dark, in a small cove in Brazil, listening to an impromptu concert between a guitarist and an opera singer who had never met, on opposite ends of an anchorage, talking to each other through music. The stars reflected on the water. Nobody spoke. We just listened.

**HOST:** How long did it go on?

**FRANCESCO:** Long enough that you never wanted it to stop. That's all I can say.

**HOST:** After seven months in Brazil — where next?

**FRANCESCO:** North. Salvador de Bahia — a city of 180 churches, African roots, Candomblé ceremonies, and a lunch in a favela that I did NOT plan. That's next episode. This is Sailing Oroboro.
