Golden Globe Race — A Serendipitous Encounter in the South Atlantic

← Back to all posts

South Atlantic Ocean, January 2019

It happened somewhere in the South Atlantic, about ten days into our crossing from St Helena to Brazil. We were alone — or so we thought. The ocean stretched empty to every horizon. No ships, no land, no nothing, just Oroboro and the sea.

Then the VHF crackled.

A voice came through with a distinctive accent — Hungarian-American, as it turned out — identifying a sailboat called Puffin. He had seen us on AIS and called us up. In the middle of the ocean, on a random morning in January, we were talking to a stranger sailing a very different race from ours.

I filmed the radio exchange on my phone. You can watch it below — it's grainy and low-fi, the way shipboard life actually is, and you can hear the static and the slight nervousness of the call (who calls who in the middle of the Atlantic?). There are subtitles because the radio compresses voices into something halfway between a human and a kettle.

What is the Golden Globe Race?

The Golden Globe Race is one of the most demanding sailing competitions ever conceived. It is a recreation of the original 1968–69 Sunday Times Golden Globe Race — the first solo non-stop circumnavigation attempt, the race that made Robin Knox-Johnston a legend. In the spirit of that event, the modern GGR bans all electronics made after 1968: no GPS, no satellite phones, no chart plotters, no email. Navigation is by sextant and paper charts. Autopilots must be mechanical or wind-driven. Communication is by SSB radio and Morse code.

The 2018 edition started on July 1, 2018, from Les Sables-d'Olonne in France. Eighteen sailors started. Only five would finish.

The race follows the classic clipper route — south past the Cape of Good Hope, across the Southern Ocean, around Cape Horn, and back north through the Atlantic to France. The distances are staggering: roughly 30,000 nautical miles, all of it solo, all of it without modern navigation aids. The boats must be classic designs built before 1988: sloops between 32 and 45 feet.

Jean-Luc Van Den Heede, a 73-year-old Frenchman, won the race in 211 days — one of the most remarkable sporting achievements of recent times. Mark Slats of the Netherlands came in second. The attrition rate was brutal: boats retired from dismasting, knockdowns, equipment failure, and exhaustion.

Identifying our visitor

At the time of the conversation, we didn't know who we were talking to. We knew it was a Golden Globe boat — the skipper mentioned the race explicitly — but there were multiple competitors still at sea in January 2019. It was only later, piecing together the clues from the conversation, that I was able to identify the sailor with certainty.

He mentioned a Windpilot wind vane self-steering — and used very specific technical language about the "wheel drum" that matched the exact terminology in the Windpilot installation documentation almost verbatim. He mentioned Whitlock Cobra rod steering. He said he had been having steering problems since Day 9 of the race, requiring emergency bearing repairs throughout the voyage. He had nearly stopped at Cape Verde. And he said he had rounded Cape Horn on January 1st, 2019 — New Year's Day — where he scattered his father's ashes.

The sailor was Istvan Kopar, a Hungarian-American sailor from New York, racing his Tradewind 36 Puffin. Every detail he shared matched the documented record of his campaign: the Windpilot sponsorship, the Cobra steering failures that plagued him from the beginning, the near-withdrawal at Cape Verde, the Cape Horn rounding on January 1st.

What happened to Puffin

After we said goodbye on the radio, Kopar continued his long journey north. He had been at sea since July 2018 and still had months ahead of him. He battled steering problems the whole way — the Whitlock Cobra system required constant attention and emergency repairs at sea — but he never gave up. He crossed the finish line at Les Sables-d'Olonne on March 21, 2019, in fourth place, after 263 days at sea.

Of the 18 who started, only 5 finished.

When we met him, he was somewhere around Day 200 of his circumnavigation — northbound in the South Atlantic, weeks away from the equator, months away from France. We had our own crossing to finish. Our tracks intersected for twenty minutes of radio chatter and then diverged forever.

The ocean is big, and the chance of two sailboats being within VHF range of each other in the middle of the South Atlantic is essentially zero. But here we were. Two boats, two very different voyages, crossing each other's wakes in the middle of nowhere. It was one of those moments that remind you why we go sailing.

We wished Istvan fair winds and signed off. Somewhere to our west, Brazil was waiting. To the north, so was the rest of the world.
